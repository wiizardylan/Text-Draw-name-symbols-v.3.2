Every Day of Peace in the Last Hundred Years is a shelter in the middle of a labyrinth. After conquering the million identical halls of A Space Gallery, here I am: inside a small gallery, floor-to-ceiling sheets cover every wall. A bowl of white school erasers sits on a little plinth in the center of the room.

Moez Surani and Nina Leo’s installation is an adaptation of Surani’s book that lists every day in the past hundred years without active interstate conflict. Only the dates of peace are recorded, with massive stretches of silence between them, entire pages completely blank. In contrast, the massive pages hung in the gallery are pencilled with every date of conflict in the last hundred years. Our job is to erase them.

I am a little crotchety on the day I go, my tote bag grinding mercilessly on my chronically-in-pain shoulder. I’m convincing myself this is totally a good thing. The event description says this piece is about grief and resistance. Maybe I can channel my cocktail of exhaustion into a kind of somber, emotional contemplation of pain.

There is a popular myth that compels us to Feel Really Bad in spaces centered around grief. Especially in western culture, violence, pain, dying are rarely depicted as funny, outside the realm of the edgelord. There are customs built around this: we solemnly post Instagram stories about loss and carefully choose a sad song, maybe even put everything in greyscale. I want to make a good impression, show my great capacity for looking pain in the face and Feeling Really Bad.

I walk into the room like it’s a funeral parlour. The kids in the gallery immediately make me look stupid for doing that. A little boy and girl have a leg up on me, doodling huge eraser marks on their respective pages. Each page of dates is probably three or four times their size. They don’t care. They’re going to town. Their dad (I assume) asks a few times if the little boy needs a ladder, but he doesn’t have time for a ladder, man, he’s busy. This has gotta be the place to be for them. Free rein to fuck shit up, in a semi-secret place no less? That never happens for kids.

I pick up an eraser and follow suit, albeit still as solemnly as I can muster.

The clock on my erasing starts ticking before I even really start. I have around fifteen minutes before I’ll have to sit down. Quiet always makes my chronic pain louder. It’s why I like techno and mosh pits. I don’t usually make my way to quiet galleries to reflect on pain. I can do that at home in bed.

Maybe my pain is at home here. I can really dig on it: I am going to Feel Bad soooo well.

One day at a time, I scrub desperately as I make a poor attempt of memorizing the dates to reference later. But the record is no match for my diligence. After five minutes, every letter and number maintains at least some ghostly legibility. I stop out of frustration, cracking my fist, shoulders, and all the other hurting parts in a way that is really satisfying but also makes the pain suck worse.

Whatever. I turn my head to see the little boy has finally accepted the POWER OF THE LADDER into his life, now unleashing the full fury of his eraser. He’s swiping out about fifteen days at a time, and I’m like, “Okay little man, you’re not gonna get very far with that strategy.”

But then again, neither am I. And I’m not having half as much fun.

I guess I forgot that grief is best served with ridiculous cutlery. Frankly, most of my memories of loss are pretty funny.

Several months after My dad, scattering the ashes from a Folger’s can, leans my dog died, 2019: over to me, whispering “Goodnight, sweet prince” à la The Big Lebowski.

Three months after One of my new roommates puts up a poster that read my house burned down, 2023: “OH FOR FUCKS SAKE, YOU FUCKING SET IT ON FIRE! FUCK!”.

May Two-Four weekend, I swear on my life my late, hilarious Uncle Bill visited 2023: me and my cousins one night via a bluetooth speaker. He ended his possession of the JBL with “The Final Countdown”.

Every dumb memory offers me a little more love to compensate for what I had to lose. Tradesies. I wonder if the path to peace is most easily forged in silliness, maybe.

The longer I spend here, the more I feel like I’m in a pillow fort with the massive white sheets and infectious glee of anarchistic children. There’s something about the softness of the space as well—the slightly-dampened echoes of how-many-times-can-I-say-why games, gentle light drifting over the texture of the paper, the smiles Leo shoots me every time I glance over at her and her kids—that make me feel like I’m inside a comforter. By now, the simultaneous performance of solemnity and pretending my real pain doesn’t exist look so ridiculous, I may as well have walked in wearing clown shoes and egg on my face. I could, maybe, try to get cozy instead.

I give up on my dream of finishing a page and look for my birthday instead. As I drop my self-serious facade, my shoulder pain gets a little easier to deal with (surprise surprise). During this self-centered Where’s Waldo, the little girl asks Leo:

WHY-GAME GIRL: What if you did it and nobody came for the show?

NINA: Well, it would be really hard. We had a lot of help.

WHY-GAME GIRL: How many people do you think came? A thousand and one?

NINA: That would be a lot of people! Probably not that many. But so many people came to help us.

I find my birthday a few times and erase it.

October 4, 2013: I take my friends to the haunted jail in Ottawa for my 11th birthday (I’m very into ghosts). My dad photoshops ghosts into all the photos because he thinks I will like it. I get so scared I cry. He laughs really hard (he has a very good laugh).

I move on to my brother’s birthday, my girlfriend’s, my best friend’s. I wonder how long it took for 9/11 to be erased (I didn’t look for it).I see the empty spaces on the wall, how they generally line up with eye level. It’s kind of cute. We start with what’s right in front of us.

Roughly one month The Wikipedia page for Penis is created. post-9/11:

Exactly two years One of my best friends in the world, Payton, is born. When we post-9/11: become roommates she tells me that when she gets hangry into Mayton and growls like a demon. She would have been invited to my birthday party at the jail in 2013 and probably liked the ghost pictures.

The popular flattening of grief into the Feel Bad Sacrifice makes this sort of thing hard to see. We are so busy looking down or away so as to perform the sacrifice correctly that the things that make pain bearable, the stuff at eye level, gets lost. Here, it gets to be the focus. At least, the gallery commentators make it easier to notice:

WHY-GAME GIRL: Which [sheet of paper] do you think is the lightest?

NINA LEO: I’m not sure!

LADDER POWER BOY: THAT ONE! He points across the room at the lightest page, for sure.

As I erase, Leo introduces herself and thanks me for my help. It makes me feel better about knowing I need to leave earlier than most people. I read online that there’s no pressure to stay for any length of time, but still, I felt I should push myself to stay as long as physically possible. Like I needed to atone for the limitation of my own pain.

It seems silly now. I am not a peace-making erasing machine. Neither is she, this artist who has achieved this immense feat of writing out thousands upon thousands of moments of violence. And now, we know each others’ names, we have touched hands, we have smiled at each other and smiled at each other and smiled at each other. It is clear this is something we are doing on purpose, together, this act of work and friendship.

I give the gallery a once-over before deciding my shoulder pain has won. Maybe you can read the days I erased close-up, but not from the door. No matter what I’ve done--what we have done--the dates of violence are still there. But they’re quieter now. We can let them go more easily. We have done the labour of looking them in the eye and said, you are something made of graphite. Your power is temporary. And that labour, sometimes, was kind of fun.

Right before I leave, I overhear the kids comparing erasers:

WHY-GAME GIRL: Yours is so dirty!!! She gasps. She is impressed.

LADDER POWER BOY: I knowww… I’ve been using it for a long time. He is ultra-proud of his dirty eraser. Yes. A thing to be proud of indeed :~).
